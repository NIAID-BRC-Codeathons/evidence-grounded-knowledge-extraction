Using LitRAG

A quick guide for exploring
pathogen literature

LitRAG helps you find biological findings reported across the literature. Start with an organism, add the gene or protein you care about, choose the kind of result you want, and inspect the papers behind each row.

1

Describe your question

Enter an organism, gene or protein, and useful terms such as “viral entry” or “isoniazid resistance.”

2

Choose a finding

Select mutations, interactions, protein functions, susceptibility results, glycosylation, or a summary.

3

Check the evidence

Read the extracted rows, open the linked citations, and download the table for follow-up work.

What literature is searched?

The backend searches a large, searchable collection built from approximately 1.5 million publications in PubMed Central. These are biomedical research articles available through the PubMed Central open-access literature resource.

LitRAG searches passages from the articles, not just titles or abstracts. A result can therefore point you to the particular text that supports a finding. The collection may be updated, so the exact set of available papers can change over time.

What should I enter?

  • Organism: use a species, pathogen, strain, or virus such as SARS-CoV-2.
  • Genes: enter one or more names separated by commas, such as Spike, ACE2 or katG, inhA.
  • Other terms: add the phenotype, disease, assay, host, or mechanism that narrows the question.
  • Use specific terms when possible. “Spike antibody escape” is more useful than “Spike” alone.

What do the data types mean?

  • Mutation: sequence changes and associated phenotypes.
  • Protein interaction: pathogen and host or protein-protein interactions.
  • Protein function: reported biological roles or activities.
  • AST: antimicrobial susceptibility results, including MIC and S/I/R when reported.
  • Glycosylation: protein sites, glycans, methods, and reported effects.
  • Summary: a prose answer when a table is not the best fit.

How should I read a result?

  • Each row is a compact statement extracted from one or more passages.
  • Support shows how many distinct sources or findings contributed to a merged row.
  • Flags call attention to unresolved citations, incomplete evidence, or values that were merged because they describe the same finding.
  • Standardized spellings help group equivalent terms; the cited paper remains the authority for the original wording.
Use results as literature leads, not as clinical or experimental conclusions. LitRAG does not replace reading the cited article. Check the passage, organism or strain, experimental method, and context before using a finding in an analysis, report, or experiment.

Practical tips

  • Begin with the default number of retrieved passages. Increase it when the topic is broad or you need a wider literature sweep.
  • Keep duplicate merging enabled for a concise table. Turn it off when you want to inspect separate extracted reports.
  • Use the citation links and source passages to distinguish measured results from predictions, reviews, or statements copied across papers.
  • Download TSV or CSV for spreadsheet review; use JSON when you need citations, passage identifiers, and provenance fields.
